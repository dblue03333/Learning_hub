ABSTRACT
Background: Geriatric assessments capture multidimensional data on physical, cognitive, psychological, and 
social health, offering opportunities to apply machine learning (ML) to support clinical decision-making in aged 
care. However, the application of ML to such data has not been systematically synthesised.
Objective: To describe the types of data used, purposes, and performance of ML models applied to multidimen-
sional geriatric assessment data.
Methods: A scoping review was conducted following the Arksey and O’Malley framework and reported using 
PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Six databases were searched for peer-reviewed studies (2012––2024) applying ML to 
multidimensional geriatric assessment data. Data were charted on study design, setting, algorithms, outcomes, 
and model performance. Methodological quality was assessed using the PROBAST + AI tool.
Results: Forty studies met inclusion criteria, most from high-income countries and community or hospital set-
tings. Frequently modelled outcomes included falls, functional decline, frailty, mortality, and hospital read-
mission. The most common data domains were comorbidities, cognition, mood, and ADL/IADL. Most studies 
(25/40) compared multiple algorithms, with the XGBoost emerging as the top-performing model in eight studies. 
Although internal validation was common, no study performed external validation. Performance metrics varied 
widely, and methodological limitations such as small sample sizes, lack of calibration, and data imbalance were 
common.
Conclusion: ML models using multidimensional geriatric assessment data show promise for predicting health 
outcomes in older adults. However, methodological and reporting limitations hinder clinical translation. Future 
research should focus on external validation, interpretability, and integration into clinical workflows to ensure 
models are robust, ethical, and applicable in real-world aged care settings.
1. Introduction
The World Health Organisation estimates that one in six people will 
be aged 60 years or older by 2030 [1]. In 2020, this demographic was 
approximately one billion people and is projected to double by 2050 [1]. 
Ageing is a risk factor for chronic conditions such as neurodegenerative, 
metabolic, cardiovascular, immune system, and musculoskeletal dis-
eases [2]. Furthermore, many older people develop multiple comor-
bidities as they age [2], and diseases in this group accounted for 23 % of 
the global disease burden [3]. As the population of older people con-
tinues to grow, the demand for health and aged care services will rise 
alongside increasing complexity in care needs, driven by 
multimorbidity, frailty, and the need for coordinated, long-term 
management.
Emerging digital technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) have 
the potential to support the management of health problems in the care 
provision for older people [4]. AI mimics human cognitive function and 
human intelligence to learn and solve real-world problems [5]. Machine 
learning (ML), a branch of AI, encompasses algorithms that enable 
computers to identify patterns within large training datasets and derive 
rules to classify or predict outcomes for new data, without explicit 
programming [6,7]. Machine learning algorithms can be categorised as 
supervised and unsupervised learning based on how they are trained 
[7]. Supervised ML models are trained on labelled datasets, where each

AI and ML techniques are increasingly being used in aged care set-
tings to address the complex physical and psychosocial needs of older 
people [5,8]. Applications include prevention of adverse events (e.g., 
prediction of falls risk, frailty, and functional decline), screening for 
common geriatric syndromes (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, mild cognitive 
impairment, and Parkinson’s disease), and supporting daily living (e.g., 
medication identification, activity monitoring, and physical exercise 
tracking) [8–10]. Furthermore, AI-powered systems are being designed 
to complement the roles traditionally held by healthcare providers, 
including rehabilitation, emotional support, social facilitation, super-
vision, and cognitive stimulation [5]. These systems often rely on 
diverse data sources such as electronic health records, sensor data, and 
medical images. However, in this study, our focus is on machine learning 
algorithms that leverage geriatric assessment data.
Geriatric assessments help to detect impairments in older people and 
facilitate development of care plans targeting those impairments. Such 
assessments can be dimension-specific (e.g., the mini-mental state ex-
amination (MMSE) for assessing cognition) or can cover multiple di-
mensions, e.g., comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), that span the 
functional, physical health, cognitive impairment and mental health, 
and psychosocial domains [11]. The rich data generated during CGA 
may be used for purposes beyond their originally intended application, 
for example, predicting outcomes using ML models [12,13]. The appli-
cation of ML in this way can identify and analyse patterns in large 
datasets with many variables, making them ideal tools for analysing 
multi-dimensional geriatric assessments characterised by complex 
interplay between geriatric syndromes and their impact on health out-
comes [5,14]. However, literature on this emerging field of using geri-
atric assessment data in machine learning models has not been clearly 
synthesised and described. Therefore, this study sought to conduct a 
scoping review to address the following research questions: 
What data from or equivalent to multi-dimensional geriatric assess-
ments have been used in machine learning (ML)?
For what purpose have such data and ML models been used?
What is the performance of the ML models for their intended 
purposes?
2. Aim
The aim of this scoping review was to describe the application of ML 
approaches utilising geriatric assessment data representing multiple 
dimensions of geriatric care in managing the health of older people.
We chose to conduct a scoping review instead of a systematic review 
and meta-analysis because the application of ML to multidimensional 
geriatric assessment data is an emerging area. To our knowledge, no 
existing reviews have comprehensively mapped the breadth and char-
acteristics of studies in this specific domain. A scoping review was 
appropriate to explore the extent, range, and nature of the available 
evidence, identify key concepts, and highlight gaps to inform future 
research: therefore, a quantitative synthesis was out of scope.
3. Materials and methods
The scoping review was conducted using the Arksey and O’Malley’s 
framework [15]. This framework consists of five stages: (1) identifying 
the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selec-
tion, (4) charting the data, and (5) collating, summarizing and reporting 
the results [15]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews 
and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) were 
used for reporting the review [16].
3.1. Eligibility criteria
We sought to include original, quantitative peer-reviewed studies 
describing the application of ML approaches to process geriatric as-
sessments data covering multiple dimensions (i.e., more than one 
dimension) in any setting (hospital, residential aged care facilities - also 
known as nursing homes, long-term care facilities, community aged 
care, home care, and primary care). We included articles that processed 
data from electronic health records (EHRs) if the data was equivalent to 
multidimensional geriatric assessment data on their own or when 
combined with other sources. For example, articles utilising EHR- 
sourced comorbidities and medications (i.e., multimorbidity and poly-
pharmacy dimensions, respectively), and combined with data from a 
mini-mental state examination (i.e., cognition dimension). We excluded 
articles with study populations aged < 65; describing ML to develop or 
conduct geriatric assessments or reporting the use of only data from 
sensors, robotics, and diagnostic tests. Reviews, qualitative studies, and 
grey literature (e.g., conference posters, abstracts, theses), and articles 
published in languages other than English or not available in full text 
were also excluded. Table 1 provides the details of the eligibility criteria 
for inclusion in the review.
3.2. Information sources and search
We searched six research databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Sci-
ence, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and ACM) using Boolean operators to 
combine relevant keywords and their associated vocabulary terms in 
each database: e.g., medical subject headings (MeSH) in PubMed. Key-
words for the search were divided into three concepts as follows: (arti-
ficial intelligence OR machine learning) AND (geriatric assessment OR 
comprehensive geriatric assessment) AND (older adult OR elderly). The 
retrieval strategy was tailored to each database as needed: e.g., the IEEE 
Xplore database does not use a vocabulary equivalent to MeSH terms 
used for PubMed. After completing the screening process, we manually 
searched the reference lists in all articles selected for inclusion to 
identify any additional relevant articles that may have been missed 
during the database searches. Searches were completed on 20 February 
2024. The full search terms are provided in supplementary material.

into Covidence software for the screening process. Two reviewers 
independently screened the search results at each stage. Titles and ab-
stracts were screened first (reviewers RD and LJ) and followed by full 
texts for detailed screening (reviewers RD and THDT). Team discussions

3.4. Data extraction and synthesis
The research team jointly developed a data extraction form. Three 
researchers (RD, SBD, AMM) independently extracted data using the 
form. Extracted data included first author, publication year, aim/ 
objective, country where the study was conducted, study design, study 
setting, sample size, sample population, geriatric assessment data used, 
method of data collection, ML techniques used, target outcomes, find-
ings, limitations and performance evaluation metrics. Inconsistencies in 
the extracted data were resolved in team discussions. We performed a 
narrative synthesis of the evidence derived from the included articles.
3.5. Quality assessment
We used the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool for 
Artificial Intelligence (PROBAST + AI) to evaluate the methodological 
quality, risk of bias, and applicability of included ML models [17]. The 
tool comprises two components: one for assessing the quality of model 
development and another for evaluating the risk of bias in model per-
formance. Each component is assessed across four domains (participants 
and data sources, predictors, outcomes, and analysis). Assessments were 
made independently by two reviewers (RD, LJ), with discrepancies 
resolved through discussion to reach consensus as recommended by the 
developers of the PROBAST + AI tool [17].
4. Results
4.1. Search and screening
A total of 964 articles were retrieved during database search. After 
removing duplicates and title/abstract screening, 593 records were 
excluded, with the top reasons being: no geriatric assessment data (324), 
wrong population (93), remote monitoring (87), and no machine 
learning (59). Seventy-eight articles were assessed for eligibility via full 
text, and 48 were excluded. An additional 10 records were identified 
through examining the reference lists of the 30 articles identified for 
inclusion through full text, resulting in 40 studies included in the final 
review (see Fig. 1).
4.2. Characteristics of included studies
Most of the studies were conducted in the USA (n = 9), followed by 
Canada (n = 5), France (n = 4), and Japan (n = 4). Other countries, 
which had three studies or less include Taiwan, Australia, Italy, Mexico, 
China, Finland, Iran, Colombia, Spain, and The Netherlands (see 
Table 2). Most studies were set in community settings (n = 21) followed 
by hospital (n = 13), home care (n = 2), and residential aged care facility 
(n = 2). One study was set in both community and residential aged care 
facility, and another study was set in multiple settings. Publication years 
ranged from 2012 to 2023, with the number of publications increasing 
over time. An overview of the included studies is provided in Table 2
while detailed characteristics for each study are provided in supple-
mentary material.

4.3. Assessment data used in ML
The studies included in this review utilised a wide array of geriatric 
assessment data spanning physical health, functional status, psycho-
logical health, and socio-environmental domains (Table 3). The most 
frequently used data types were medical history and comorbidities, 
depression and mood disorders, cognitive impairment, ADL/IADL, fall 
history, nutrition and mobility.
In most studies, data were sourced from a combination of electronic 
health records, surveys, and health assessments. A total of 20 studies 
used multiple instruments to conduct geriatric assessments while 16 
studies used comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) instruments. 
The Resident Assessment Instrument (RAI) was the most common CGA 
instrument for data collection, and six studies used the RAI or the 
interRAI as their only data source. The instrument was also used in 
combination with other sources, including ambulatory and discharge 
databases [58] and a smart device [56] to capture physical activity, 
heart rate and sleep times. Six studies used healthcare databases, such as 
Medicare and emergency department databases as the only sources of 
data for ML models. One study [28] did not report how data was 
collected.
4.4. Purpose of ML models
Results indicate that geriatric assessment data are commonly used in 
machine learning models to predict a range of health outcomes, 
including falls, functional status, mortality, sarcopenia, in-hospital 
complications, cognitive function, post-discharge outcomes, and gen-
eral health. ML approaches are also applied to aged care service-related 
outcomes such as emergency department visits, nursing home admis-
sion, rehabilitation use, and to identify patient characteristics or explore 
correlations between assessments and daily activity or sleep. A wide 
range of algorithms were used, with logistic regression and random 
forest being the most frequent, particularly in fall prediction. Falls were 
the most common target outcome, with diverse algorithms applied, 
reflecting a preference for ensemble-based models rather than a single 
dominant approach. Most studies evaluated multiple algorithms to 
identify the best-performing model, while those using a single algorithm 
lacked justification for their choice, though selections were generally 
appropriate for the study aims: e.g., the use of K-means clustering to 
group centenarian client characteristics [29].
4.5. Quality assessment
Table 4 summarises the overall judgements for the model develop-
ment and evaluations components, while Fig. 2 summarises the indi-
vidual domains for each component (details for individual PROBAST +
AI assessment are provided in supplementary material).
4.5.1. Model development
The overall quality for model development for most studies was 
judged as ‘Unclear’ (75 %) because they did not provide evidence of 
applying calibration techniques for the models: only four studies report 
use of calibration techniques [40,52,55,57] (reflected in the Analysis 
domain of the Development component in Fig. 2). Furthermore, only 16 
studies reported the application of techniques to reduce the effects of 
class imbalance, with only three using advanced techniques such as 
synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) [20,33,45] and the 
rest using a variety of simple techniques such as resampling, balanced 
class weights and cross validation. The use of these class imbalance 
mitigation techniques improved the performance metrics of the models.
Only three studies implemented model interpretation techniques to 
explain individual fall risk predictions [33,40,45,57], all of which used

4.6. Performance of ML models
Thirty articles used internal validation to evaluate the performance 
of ML models in their studies. Only two studies evaluated ML model 
performance by benchmarking against traditional statistical prediction 
tools, with ML models producing comparable [38] or superior results 
[44]. The remaining studies assessed performance by conducting in-
ternal validation using standard ML evaluation metrics such as accuracy, 
sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, positive predictive value (PPV), nega-
tive predictive value (NPV), and area under the receiver operating 
characteristic curve (AUC). These metrics were applied inconsistently 
across studies, with studies selecting various combinations based on 
suitability and objectives. For an overview of these metrics and their 
relevance in medical applications, see Rainio et al [60].
Results of performance evaluations were mixed. Some models ach-
ieved very high values across several metrics, while others showed only 
moderate performance. For example, Sullivan et al. reported moderate 
AUC (0.71–0.74), sensitivity (64–73 %), and specificity (58–70 %) for 
mortality prediction using three models [51]. In contrast, Qiao et al. 
achieved a high AUC of 0.92 and demonstrated superior performance 
compared to traditional statistical models [44]. Notably, even within 
individual studies, models often showed high performance on some 
metrics but low performance on others. Ambagtsheer et al., for instance, 
reported high accuracy (93.5 %), sensitivity (97.8 %), specificity (89.1 
%), positive agreement (90 %), and Cohen’s Kappa (0.87) but the lowest 
negative agreement (2.4 %) among all models evaluated [18]. Direct 
comparisons across studies were challenging due to the variability in 
metrics and evaluation methods. However, among the 29 studies that 
reported accuracy, 19 (65.5 %) achieved high accuracy with their best- 
performing models, while the remainder reported moderate accuracy. 
These results were observed across diverse settings, target outcomes, 
and ML algorithms, suggesting that performance was not linked to 
specific algorithms, contexts or assessment. Interestingly, several studies 
noted improved performance when models were trained on larger and 
more comprehensive datasets [18,34,44,50,55,56]. For example, Imura 
et al [34] found that combining basic demographic, functional, and 
environmental variables significantly enhanced the model’s ability to 
predict discharge outcomes after stroke rehabilitation.
Among the 25 studies that compared multiple machine learning al-
gorithms, XGBoost was the top-performing model in eight cases, making 
it the most frequently identified best performer. These studies applied 
XGBoost to predict outcomes such as functional status, falls, mobility, 
and mortality, demonstrating its robustness and adaptability across 
diverse domains. However, some studies used small sample sizes, lacked 
proper separation between training and test datasets, or did not address 
the potential risks of overfitting or data leakage. Notably, none of the 
included studies conducted long-term evaluations to assess the real- 
world impact of ML models on health outcomes in geriatric care; all 
focused solely on model development and internal validation metrics.
5. Discussion
5.1. Main findings
This scoping review identified and synthesized evidence from 40 
studies applying machine learning (ML) to multidimensional geriatric 
assessment data. The findings show that ML models are increasingly 
used to predict a wide range of health outcomes in older adults, 
including falls, functional decline, mortality, and hospital readmissions. 
The most used assessment domains were medical history, cognitive 
function, ADL/IADL, fall history, and nutrition: reflecting the complex 
nature of ageing and the value of multidimensional data in capturing 
this complexity. Among studies comparing multiple algorithms, 
XGBoost consistently achieved the highest performance metrics across 
diverse outcomes such as function, falls, mobility, and mortality. This is 
likely due to its ensemble learning approach, which sequentially builds 
decision trees using gradient descent to minimize error, resulting in 
improved predictive accuracy [61–65]. Model performance varied 
widely across studies, though most reported high accuracy. However, all 
evaluations were based solely on internal validation, and thus, perfor-
mance metrics should be interpreted with caution in the absence of 
external or real-world validation.
5.2. Challenges and implications for policy, practice and research
The review also highlights several key challenges to the development 
and deployment of machine learning (ML) models in aged care. First, the 
heterogeneity of assessment tools limits comparability across studies. 
While some studies employed comprehensive instruments such as the 
interRAI systems, inconsistencies in data collection methods hinder 
model generalizability. Standardizing assessment tools and data formats 
are therefore essential to enable cross-study comparisons and support 
model portability [66]. In addition to variability in data sources, the use 
of small and homogenous samples in model development presents 
another barrier to generalizability. Most studies were conducted in high- 
income countries and trained models on local, setting-specific pop-
ulations. This increases the risk of overfitting and bias, and limits the 
applicability of these models to other contexts, particularly in under-
represented or resource-constrained aged care settings. However, 
caution is warranted in the pursuit of generalizability. Some ML models 
rely on variables that are highly specific to the target population, care 
setting, or clinical condition, and may weight these variables dispro-
portionately [21–23,25,27,33,34,37,39–43,45]. For example, models

condition-specific features such as disease duration or severity, which 
may not be relevant to broader geriatric populations. Consequently, 
such models may perform poorly when applied outside their original 
context. Given these limitations, a pragmatic approach may be to 
continue developing application-specific models tailored to well-defined 
subgroups. These models can offer greater clinical relevance and accu-
racy within their intended context, while future work can explore stra-
tegies for modularity, adaptation, and transfer learning to support 
broader applicability.
Second, although less widespread in the included studies, data 
quality issues can potentially limit the reliability of predictions [67]. 
Some studies in this review relied on retrospective data from small study 
populations. Self-reported reported variables were also used, which may 
introduce risk of recall bias, especially in geriatric care settings where 
cognitive conditions are common [35,43]. Handling of missing data in a 
few studies was suboptimal or not reported, with simple approaches 
such as variable exclusion or default imputation being applied, instead 
of more robust techniques like multiple imputation. Moreover, imbal-
anced datasets (e.g., datasets with disproportionately more non-fallers 
than fallers) can reduce model performance [35,40,55] but less than 
half of included studies reported applying mitigating techniques.
Third, methodological limitations in model development and eval-
uation were widespread. Most notably, external validation of ML models 
in this field are lacking, with studies relying only on internal validation. 
Moreover, in some cases, these internal validations were conducted with 
very small sample sizes. High performance values in internal validations 
do not always equate to clinical utility, especially when models are 
complex and opaque [18,58]. The limited reporting of calibration 
metrics such as Brier scores, calibration plots, and slopes, limits confi-
dence in predictive reliability. Similarly, the failure to incorporate 
interpretability in model development produces ‘black-box’ applications 
that reduce clinical utility.
These challenges must be addressed for the potential of ML and 
geriatric assessments to have clinical value. The following are recom-
mended high priority areas for further work in this field.
5.2.1. Recommendations
First, a consistent emphasis must be placed on external validation 
across independent cohorts using larger multi-centre, national, or lon-
gitudinal datasets to ensure generalisability and reproducibility of 
model performance. This must also include temporal validation and the 
use of prospective evaluation to assess model stability over time and 
across populations. Complementary to this, decision-analytic evalua-
tions, such as net-benefit and decision-curve analysis, must be explored 
to assess ML model influence on clinical or policy decisions and their 
downstream consequences. This is particularly relevant in geriatric care, 
where resource constraints and individualized care goals necessitate 
careful consideration of clinical utility and cost-effectiveness.
Second, model calibration and comprehensive performance report-
ing still require greater attention. Studies must aim to adhere to routine 
reporting of calibration plots and statistics alongside discrimination 
metrics, as well as transparent descriptions of feature selection, hyper- 
parameter tuning, and model optimisation procedures: we encourage 
the use of reporting frameworks such as TRIPOD-AI [68] and PROBAST 
+ AI [17] to promote transparency, comparability and reproducibility.
Third, interpretability techniques must be incorporated into predic-
tion models. Improving model interpretability is critical to bridging the 
gap between technical performance and clinical adoption. Clinicians 
have been reported to be hesitant in adopting ML tools that lack trans-
parency or are difficult to interpret [69–72]. While many ML models 
demonstrated high predictive accuracy, their complexity would make 
them difficult for clinicians to understand and trust. Techniques such as 
SHAP, LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), and 
decision tree visualisations are increasingly being used to make model 
outputs more transparent and explainable [73–77] but were only 
applied by three studies in this review. Interpretable ML models allow 
clinicians to see most influential features in predictions, fostering

Finally, integrating ML into clinical workflows will require not only

5.3. Strengths and limitations
This review conducted a comprehensive search of multiple databases 
and manual reference searches to maximise the likelihood of capturing 
relevant articles in the literature. However, the review only included 
studies conducted on people aged 65 + despite that in some contexts, 
particularly in low-income settings where life expectancy is lower, 
people aged 55 + or 60 + are considered elderly. The exclusion of the 
studies conducted on people aged < 65 and grey literature may limit the 
review findings.
6. Conclusion
In conclusion, the field of ML models using multidimensional geri-
atric data is still in the early stages (as evidenced by the focus on model 
development and internal evaluation in all included studies as well as 
experimentation with various ML algorithms, and datasets). Several 
challenges will need further research before ML using geriatric data can 
be implemented into clinical practice. Sustained interdisciplinary 
collaboration will also be essential to explore the impact of health out-
comes to fully realise the potential of ML in geriatric care. Clinicians, 
data scientists, informaticians, aged care practitioners, and policy-
makers must work together to ensure that ML models are not only 
technically robust but also clinically meaningful, ethical, and practically 
implementable.
